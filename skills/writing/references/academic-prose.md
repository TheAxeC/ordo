# Academic prose

These rules hold for academic text, alongside the prose standard (`references/prose-standard.md`). Where the prose standard already states a rule, the page names its section. The `writing` skill and the skills built on it read a manuscript's prose against this page.

## Core principles

- **Specific terms.** Use the most specific term available.
- **Definitions.** Define each technical term at its first use.
- **Antecedents.** Use a pronoun such as "this" or "it" only where its antecedent is clear.
- **One idea per sentence.** Give each sentence one idea, or ideas that are clearly connected.
- **Filler.** Prose standard, section A, holds the filler words, and the wordy forms below are the redundant phrases to cut.
- **Sentence length.** Prose standard, section E, holds the sentence-length rule.
- **Evidence.** Base each claim on evidence rather than opinion.
- **Hedging.** Hedge an uncertain claim, on the scale under Hedging.
- **Limitations.** Acknowledge the limitations of a claim and the alternative interpretations of its evidence.
- **Full forms.** Write the full form: "do not" rather than "don't".
- **Formal vocabulary.** Use formal academic vocabulary, and keep colloquialisms and slang for informal writing.
- **Person.** Write in the third person unless the discipline's conventions allow the first person.

## Engineering and CS register

Text in engineering and computer science takes this register:

- **Register.** Formal, oriented to a problem and its solution, and precise to the specification.
- **Voice.** Passive voice is common for methods, and active voice for contributions. In a method step the actor is usually irrelevant, the case where prose standard, section E, allows the passive.
- **Terminology.** Technical specifications and performance metrics.
- **Example.** "The proposed algorithm achieves O(n log n) complexity, outperforming the baseline by 34% on the benchmark dataset."

## Hedging

A hedge marks how strongly the evidence supports a claim, from weak to strong.

| Strength | Hedging devices | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Weak | may, might, could, possibly | "This may suggest a correlation." |
| Moderate | suggests, indicates, appears | "The data suggest a positive trend." |
| Strong | demonstrates, establishes, confirms | "The evidence demonstrates a clear link." |

### When to hedge

Hedge a claim in these cases:

- A result that needs replication.
- A causal claim from correlational data.
- A generalization from a limited sample.
- An interpretation that has alternative explanations.

### When not to hedge

Do not hedge in these cases:

- **Reported data.** "The response rate was 78%." rather than "appeared to be".
- **Method.** "Thematic analysis was used." rather than "An attempt was made to use thematic analysis.".
- **Established facts.** "Earth orbits the Sun." rather than "may orbit".

## Transition words

A transition names the relation between two sentences or paragraphs. Choose the word by the relation.

| Relation | Words |
|---|---|
| Addition | moreover, furthermore, in addition, additionally, similarly, likewise |
| Contrast | however, nevertheless, in contrast, on the other hand, conversely, whereas |
| Cause and effect | therefore, consequently, as a result, thus, hence, accordingly |
| Example | for example, for instance, specifically, in particular, such as, namely |
| Sequence | first, second, third, subsequently, finally, meanwhile |
| Summary | in summary, to conclude, overall, taken together, in short |
| Concession | although, despite, while, granted that, notwithstanding |

## Paragraph shape

An academic paragraph runs in this order:

1. **Topic sentence.** It states the paragraph's main point.
2. **Evidence.** Data, citations and examples that support the point.
3. **Explanation.** It interprets the evidence and connects it to the argument.
4. **Link.** It connects to the next paragraph or back to the thesis.

Prose standard, section D, holds the rules on a paragraph's idea and length. An example, with each part marked:

> [T] AI-assisted quality assurance has shown promise in improving evaluation consistency across institutions. [E] Smith (2024) found that institutions using AI tools reported a 23% reduction in inter-rater variance, while Chen and Wang (2023) documented improved agreement on scoring rubrics (kappa = 0.82 against 0.64). [E] These findings suggest that algorithmic assistance can mitigate the subjective biases inherent in human evaluation, particularly when assessors have varying levels of experience. [L] However, the reliance on AI tools also raises concerns about the loss of contextual judgment, which the following section addresses.

## Wordy forms

| Wordy | Concise |
|---|---|
| due to the fact that | because |
| a large number of | the number itself (prose standard, section A) |
| at the present time | currently, or now |
| in the event that | if |
| has the ability to | can |
| with regard to | regarding, or about |
| in spite of the fact that | despite, or although |
| conduct an investigation of | investigate |

Prose standard, section C, covers the wordy opener "in order to". `references/anti-patterns.md` holds "it is important to note that".

## Vague forms

| Vague | Precise |
|---|---|
| "many studies" | the studies named and cited, such as "several studies (e.g., Chen, 2023; Smith, 2024)" |
| "a significant impact" | the measured figure, such as "a 23% increase in retention rates" |
| "in recent years" | the year or the span, such as "since 2020" or "over the past five years" |
| "some researchers" | the researchers named, with citations |
| "it is well known that" | a citation of the source, or nothing |

## Tense per section

| Section | Tense | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Literature review, reporting findings | Past | "Smith (2024) found that..." |
| Literature review, the ongoing state | Present | "The theory posits that..." |
| Method | Past | "Data were collected through..." |
| Results | Past | "The analysis revealed..." |
| Discussion, interpreting | Present | "These findings suggest..." |
| Conclusion, implications | Present or future | "This has implications for..." or "Future research should..." |

## Quotations

A quotation keeps its source's punctuation, so a dash inside it is left to be judged by hand.
