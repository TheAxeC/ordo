# Academic writing rules

These rules cover academic prose where the prose standard is silent. The prose standard holds wherever the two differ. A heading marked (manuscripts and grants) applies only to a manuscript or a grant. A finding names a rule by its group and its heading, as in "Precision / Term defined at first use".

## Precision

### Most specific term

A passage names each thing with the most specific term the field has for it.

### Term defined at first use

A technical term is defined where it first appears.

### Pronoun with a clear antecedent

A pronoun such as "this" or "it" has a clear antecedent. A paragraph that opens with a pronoun that has no antecedent falls under the prose standard, section E, "Cold opens".

## Concision

### One idea per sentence (manuscripts and grants)

A sentence carries one idea, or clearly connected ideas.

## Objectivity

### Claims rest on evidence

A claim rests on evidence, not on opinion.

### Limitations and alternative interpretations

The text acknowledges the limitations of the study and the alternative interpretations of its results.

## Formality (manuscripts and grants)

### Full forms

A contraction is written out: "do not" for "don't".

### No colloquial words

The vocabulary is formal and academic. A colloquialism or a slang word belongs to informal writing.

## Hedging

### Hedge strength

The hedging scale has three strengths, each with its devices.

| Strength | Hedging devices | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Weak | may, might, could, possibly | "This may suggest a correlation." |
| Moderate | suggests, indicates, appears | "The data suggest a positive trend." |
| Strong | demonstrates, establishes, confirms | "The evidence demonstrates a clear link." |

### Hedge where a claim is uncertain

A claim of one of these four sorts is hedged:

- A result that needs replication.
- A causal claim from correlational data.
- A generalization from a limited sample.
- An interpretation with alternative explanations.

### No hedge on facts

Reported data, the description of a method and a well-established fact are stated without a hedge.

| Passage that hedges a fact | Passage that states it |
|---|---|
| "The response rate appeared to be 78%." | "The response rate was 78%." |
| "We attempted to use thematic analysis." | "We used thematic analysis." |
| "The Earth may orbit the Sun." | "The Earth orbits the Sun." |

## Transitions

### Transition that states the relation

A transition word states the relation its sentence has to the sentence before it. A transition whose relation the two sentences do not have is cut.

| Relation | Transitions |
|---|---|
| Addition | moreover, furthermore, in addition, additionally, similarly, likewise |
| Contrast | however, nevertheless, in contrast, on the other hand, conversely, whereas |
| Cause and effect | therefore, consequently, as a result, thus, hence, accordingly |
| Example | for example, for instance, specifically, in particular, such as, namely |
| Sequence | first, second, third, subsequently, finally, meanwhile |
| Summary | in summary, to conclude, overall, taken together, in short |
| Concession | although, despite, while, granted that, notwithstanding |

## Paragraphs

### First sentence states the point

A paragraph's first sentence states the paragraph's main point.

### Claim with evidence or citation

A claim in a paragraph is followed by the data, the citation or the example that supports it.

### Evidence followed by what it means

Evidence in a paragraph is followed by an interpretation that connects it to the paragraph's claim.

## Common style errors

### Wordy phrase

A wordy phrase is replaced with its concise form. The prose standard, section C, holds "in order to".

| Wordy | Concise |
|---|---|
| due to the fact that | because |
| a large number of | the stated number |
| at the present time | currently, now |
| it is important to note that | the phrase deleted |
| in the event that | if |
| has the ability to | can |
| with regard to | regarding, about |
| in spite of the fact that | despite, although |
| conduct an investigation of | investigate |

### Vague phrase

A vague phrase is replaced with the specific claim.

| Vague | Specific |
|---|---|
| "many studies" | "several studies (e.g., Chen, 2023; Smith, 2024)" |
| "a significant impact" | "a 23% increase in retention rates" |
| "in recent years" | "since 2020" or "over the past five years" |
| "some researchers" | the researchers named, with citations |
| "it is well known that" | the source cited, or the phrase removed |

### Tense per section

A section reports in the tense of its row.

| Section | Tense | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Literature review, reporting results | Past | "Smith (2024) found that..." |
| Literature review, ongoing state | Present | "The theory posits that..." |
| Methodology | Past | "Data were collected through..." |
| Results | Past | "The analysis revealed..." |
| Discussion, interpreting | Present | "These results suggest that..." |
| Conclusion, implications | Present or future | "This has implications for..." or "Future research should..." |

## Reader's path

### Clarity test

The test is whether the paper still makes sense with the paragraph removed. A paragraph whose removal loses nothing is deleted.

### Reader's four questions

At any point the reader can answer four questions. A sentence that announces what the page is about to do falls under the prose standard, section C.

1. Where am I? The section structure and signposting answer it.
2. Why am I here? The connection to the research question answers it.
3. What should I take away? The point of the section answers it.
4. Where am I going next? The transition logic answers it.

### So what per section

A section answers the reader's question in its row and does the job in its row.

| Section | The reader's question | The section's job |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Why should I care about this topic? | Opens with a real-world consequence or a gap in knowledge. |
| Literature review | What is missing from what we know? | Builds to the gap and does more than summarize. |
| Methodology | Can I trust these results? | Shows rigor and acknowledges limitations upfront. |
| Results | What did you actually find? | Leads with the result, not the statistical test. |
| Discussion | What does this mean for the field? | Connects back to the gap and states what the study adds to what was known. |
| Conclusion | What should I remember? | Gives one sentence that captures the contribution. |

## Flagged terms

The prose standard, section A, flags these terms. A finding on one asks for the most precise word in its place.

### Alternatives

This table is reference for the skill that writes a fix. No finding is reported under it, and no finding lists an alternative.

| Term | Why it is flagged | Alternatives, by context |
|---|---|---|
| delve | Stands in for "explore" | examine, investigate, analyze, explore |
| tapestry | Cliche metaphor for complexity | network, interplay, system |
| landscape | Vague when it is not literal | field, domain, context, state of |
| pivotal | Inflates importance | important, central, key |
| crucial | Inflates importance | essential, necessary, critical, vital |
| foster | Vague verb | promote, develop, cultivate, encourage |
| showcase | Non-academic register | demonstrate, illustrate, present, reveal |
| testament | Cliche | evidence, indicator, demonstration |
| navigate | Vague when it is not literal | manage, address, handle, negotiate |
| leverage | Business jargon | use, employ, utilize, apply |
| realm | Archaic and poetic | domain, field, area, sphere |
| embark | Overwrought for "begin" | begin, initiate, undertake, start |
| underscore | Overused emphasis verb | emphasize, highlight, stress, reinforce |
| multifaceted | Claims complexity without saying what it is | complex, varied, diverse, multilayered |
| nuanced | Empty unless the nuance is stated | subtle, detailed, fine-grained, qualified |
| comprehensive | Claims completeness without showing it | thorough, extensive, broad, detailed |
| robust | Vague quality claim | reliable, strong, rigorous, resilient |
| intricate | Same fault as "multifaceted" | complex, detailed, elaborate, involved |
| cornerstone | Cliche metaphor | foundation, basis, core element, pillar |
| paradigm | Overused outside the philosophy of science | framework, model, approach |
| synergy | Business jargon | interaction, cooperation, combined effect |
| holistic | Vague without a definition | integrated, whole-system |
| streamline | Non-academic | simplify, optimize, improve efficiency |
| cutting-edge | Cliche | recent, advanced, state-of-the-art, novel |
| groundbreaking | Inflates importance | novel, innovative, pioneering, original |

### Discipline exceptions

A flagged term that is standard terminology in the draft's discipline is not reported:

- "paradigm shift" in the philosophy of science.
- "landscape" in its literal sense in ecology or geography.
- "robust estimator" in statistics.
- "navigate" in its literal sense in wayfinding research.
