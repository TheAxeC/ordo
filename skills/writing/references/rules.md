# Academic prose rules

These rules add to the prose standard what it lacks. They apply to a manuscript, a grant or any other academic draft. A rule about a section of a paper applies to a draft that has that section. Where a rule here and the prose standard differ, the prose standard holds. A finding names a rule by this page and its section, such as `references/rules.md`, "Hedging".

## Terms defined at first use

- A technical term is defined where it first appears. A draft that uses it before it defines it, or never defines it, is reported.
- The replacement adds the definition, or names the fact the author must supply.

## Most specific term

- The most specific term available is used. A general word where a specific term exists is reported, with the specific term as the replacement.

## Clear antecedents

- A pronoun such as "this" or "it" with no clear antecedent is reported. The replacement names the noun the pronoun stands for.

## Hedging

A hedge matches the strength of the evidence.

| Strength | Devices | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Weak | may, might, could, possibly | "This may suggest a correlation." |
| Moderate | suggests, indicates, appears | "The data suggest a positive trend." |
| Strong | demonstrates, establishes, confirms | "The evidence demonstrates a clear link." |

- A claim of one of these kinds is hedged:
  - a result that needs replication;
  - a causal claim from correlational data;
  - a generalization from a limited sample;
  - an interpretation with alternative explanations.
- Such a claim stated without a hedge, or with a strong device the evidence does not carry, is reported. The replacement uses a device of the strength the evidence carries.
- A claim of one of these kinds is not hedged:
  - reported factual data, such as "The response rate was 78%." in place of "The response rate appeared to be 78%.";
  - a description of the method, such as "We used thematic analysis." in place of "We attempted to use thematic analysis.";
  - a well-established fact, such as "The Earth orbits the Sun." in place of "The Earth may orbit the Sun."
- A hedge on a claim of one of those kinds is reported and cut.

## Limitations and alternative interpretations

- A claim whose evidence allows another explanation, or holds only within a limit, is reported when the draft states neither. The replacement adds the alternative or the limit, or names the fact the author must supply.

## Evidence for claims

- A claim with neither evidence nor a citation is reported. The replacement adds the evidence or the citation, or names the fact the author must supply.

## Paragraph point

- A paragraph whose first sentence does not state its point is reported. The replacement is a first sentence that states it.

## Explanation of evidence

- Evidence that is not followed by what it means for the paragraph's claim is reported. The replacement adds a sentence that states the meaning.

## Transitions

A transition states the relation a sentence has to the one before it. A transition whose relation the two sentences do not have is reported, and the replacement cuts it.

| Relation | Transitions |
|---|---|
| Addition | moreover, furthermore, in addition, additionally, similarly, likewise |
| Contrast | however, nevertheless, in contrast, on the other hand, conversely, whereas |
| Cause and effect | therefore, consequently, as a result, thus, hence, accordingly |
| Example | for example, for instance, specifically, in particular, such as, namely |
| Sequence | first, second, third, subsequently, finally, meanwhile |
| Summary | in summary, to conclude, overall, taken together, in short |
| Concession | although, despite, while, granted that, notwithstanding |

- A summary transition that opens a sentence restating the paragraph is reported under the prose standard, section E, and the sentence is cut.

## Tense per section

A sentence whose tense differs from the row for its section and use is reported. The replacement puts it in the tense of the row.

| Section | Tense | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Literature review, reporting findings | Past | "Smith (2024) found that..." |
| Literature review, an ongoing state | Present | "The theory posits that..." |
| Methodology | Past | "Data were collected through..." |
| Results | Past | "The analysis revealed..." |
| Discussion, interpreting | Present | "These findings suggest..." |
| Conclusion, implications | Present or future | "This has implications for... / Future research should..." |

## Clarity test

The test asks of each paragraph whether the draft still makes sense without it.

- A paragraph whose removal loses nothing is reported. The replacement deletes it.
- A paragraph whose removal loses only context stays. A paragraph whose removal breaks the argument stays. Neither is reported under this section.

## Reader's four questions

At every point of the draft a reader can answer four questions. A passage after which one of them has no answer in the text is reported, and the finding names the question.

- Where am I? The headings and the paragraph's own content answer it. A sentence that announces what the section does does not, since the prose standard, section C, cuts it.
- Why am I here? The text connects the passage to the research question.
- What should I take away? The passage makes its point.
- Where am I going next? The first sentence of the next paragraph states its relation to the one before, as "Transitions" says.

## Reader's question per section

A section whose text does not answer its reader's question is reported. The replacement does what the last column says.

| Section | The reader asks | The section does |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Why should I care about this topic? | Opens with a real-world consequence or a knowledge gap |
| Literature review | What is missing from what we know? | Builds to the gap, and does more than summarize |
| Methodology | Can I trust these results? | Shows rigor and states the limitations up front |
| Results | What did you find? | Leads with the finding, before the statistical test |
| Discussion | What does this mean for the field? | Connects back to the gap and states the difference the work makes |
| Conclusion | What should I remember? | States the contribution in one sentence |

## Wordiness

A phrase of the left column is reported. The replacement is the right column.

| Phrase | Replacement |
|---|---|
| in order to | to |
| due to the fact that | because |
| a large number of | the number itself |
| at the present time | now or currently |
| it is important to note that | deleted |
| in the event that | if |
| has the ability to | can |
| with regard to | about or regarding |
| in spite of the fact that | although or despite |
| conduct an investigation of | investigate |

- Where the draft gives no number for "a large number of", the replacement names the number the author must supply.

## Vague language

A phrase of the left column is reported. The replacement is the right column.

| Phrase | Replacement |
|---|---|
| "many studies" | The studies named with their citations, or their count |
| "a significant impact" | The measured effect, such as "a 23% increase in retention rates" |
| "in recent years" | A dated span, such as "since 2020" or "over the past five years" |
| "some researchers" | The researchers named with their citations |
| "it is well known that" | The citation of the source, or nothing when no source exists |

## Replacements for flagged terms

The prose standard, section A, flags the terms of the left column. When a use is not the most precise word, the replacement is one of the right column, whichever fits the sentence.

| Term | Replacements |
|---|---|
| delve | examine, investigate, analyze, explore |
| tapestry | network, interplay, system |
| landscape | field, domain, context, state of |
| pivotal | important, central, key |
| crucial | essential, necessary, critical, vital |
| foster | promote, develop, cultivate, encourage |
| showcase | demonstrate, illustrate, present, reveal |
| testament | evidence, indicator, demonstration |
| navigate | manage, address, handle, negotiate |
| leverage | use, employ, utilize, apply |
| realm | domain, field, area, sphere |
| embark | begin, initiate, undertake, start |
| underscore | emphasize, highlight, stress, reinforce |
| multifaceted | complex, varied, diverse, multilayered |
| nuanced | subtle, detailed, fine-grained, qualified |
| comprehensive | thorough, extensive, broad, detailed |
| robust | reliable, strong, rigorous, resilient |
| intricate | complex, detailed, elaborate, involved |
| cornerstone | foundation, basis, core element, pillar |
| paradigm | framework, model, approach |
| synergy | interaction, cooperation, combined effect |
| holistic | integrated, whole-system |
| streamline | simplify, optimize, improve efficiency |
| cutting-edge | recent, advanced, state-of-the-art, novel |
| groundbreaking | novel, innovative, pioneering, original |

- A term that is standard terminology in the discipline the draft addresses is not reported:
  - "paradigm shift" in the philosophy of science;
  - "landscape" in ecology or geography, when it is literal;
  - "robust" in statistics, as in "robust estimator";
  - "navigate" in wayfinding research, when it is literal.
- A replacement is never itself a flagged term of the prose standard, section A, or one of its vague qualifiers. A replacement that would be one is left out of the table above, and a finding does not propose it.

## Formality

These rules apply in a manuscript or a grant.

- A contraction is reported. The replacement is the full form, such as "do not" for "don't".
- A colloquial word or a slang term is reported. The replacement is the formal academic word.
- A sentence that carries two ideas that could stand alone is reported. The replacement splits it. Clearly connected ideas may share a sentence.
