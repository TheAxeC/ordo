An interpretable block-term tensor network with a
separate clinical term for predicting incident atrial
fibrillation from one sinus-rhythm 12-lead ECG

Abstract
Atrial fibrillation (AF) raises stroke risk, and electrocardiogram (ECG) models that predict its onset are explained only after training, by attribution
methods that agree poorly. We asked whether a model whose ECG evidence
passes through readable factors predicts incident AF from one sinus-rhythm
12-lead ECG more accurately than a clinical model and a convolutional neural network (CNN). We also asked where in the cardiac cycle its evidence
lies. We propose the block-term tensor network (BTTN), a Cox survival
network on a frequency by time by lead spectrogram tensor. Each block
projects the tensor onto a frequency factor and a lead factor, and age, sex
and ECG intervals enter a separate linear clinical term that bypasses these
factors. Incident AF was defined from coded diagnoses in the MIMIC-IV
hospital records linked to MIMIC-IV-ECG. At one year, BTTN with the
clinical term had the highest area under the receiver operating characteristic
curve (AUROC) of the methods compared, 0.7776 (95% confidence interval
[0.7664, 0.7888]). The clinical model reached 0.7597 [0.7488, 0.7713] and the
CNN 0.7350 [0.7226, 0.7469]. The ECG term alone reached 0.7397 [0.7275,
0.7517], comparable to the CNN. The AUROCs of BTTN with the clinical
term ranged from 0.723 to 0.745 at horizons from 120 days to 5 years after
the ECG, with a 30-day blanking window. These values were computed from
one set of cross-validated survival risks, without refitting for any horizon. In
the survival sample, which over-represents patients with AF, observed oneyear AF rose from 0.55% in the lowest risk decile to 11.4% in the highest.

1. Introduction
1.1. Context and problem
Atrial fibrillation (AF) is a common arrhythmia, and in people with
asymptomatic AF the first presentation may be a stroke or heart failure
[1]. In the Framingham Heart Study, AF was associated with a near fivefold
excess of stroke. The stroke risk attributable to AF rose from 1.5% at ages
50 to 59 to 23.5% at ages 80 to 89 [2]. Over 50 years of the Framingham
Heart Study, the age-adjusted prevalence of AF rose about fourfold and its
incidence more than tripled, in part through more intensive surveillance [3].
Identifying the people who will develop AF could therefore guide monitoring
before a first complication. Clinical risk scores such as CHARGE-AF estimate this risk from routine variables [4]. These scores need the abstraction
of clinical data, which takes time, whereas a model applied to a single ECG
at the point of care needs no additional data abstraction [1]. The resting 12lead electrocardiogram (ECG) is thus a candidate source of risk information
in its own right.
Deep networks trained on sinus-rhythm ECGs predict new-onset AF. A
network trained on 1.6 million ECGs reached an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.85 for new-onset AF within one
year [5]. When an ECG network and CHARGE-AF were combined, discrimination was highest for the combination in an internal and two external test
sets [6]. The ECG and the clinical risk factors therefore carry partly different information. Reported performance also depends on how the outcome is
defined. A systematic review of 14 studies found a median AUC of 0.740,
with higher values for 31-day confirmation windows than for 1-year windows
(0.851 against 0.630) [7]. Eleven of these studies cited possible misclassification of negative cases. A model of incident AF therefore has to be judged
with its outcome window and its label source stated.

<U+000C>1.2. Research gap
The networks behind these results are black boxes, and their explanations are computed after training. A commentary on predicting incident
AF in sinus rhythm is subtitled <left double quotation mark>more than just trusting the <left single quotation mark>black box<right single quotation mark> <right double quotation mark>
[1], and a narrative review names interpretability as a barrier to clinical use
[8]. The 2024 guideline of the European Society of Cardiology addresses the
neighbouring task of automated AF diagnosis. It states that the clinical performance and broad applicability of such algorithms are not yet known [9].
The explanations themselves are uncertain. Twelve attribution methods applied to ECG classifiers agreed poorly and were only moderately consistent
across training runs for most methods [10]. For some of these methods, randomising the weights of the network did not remove the attribution pattern
entirely. An explanation of this kind may describe the attribution method
as much as the model. Rudin argued that a model for high-stakes decisions
should be interpretable by design, instead of a black box explained afterwards
[11]. For incident AF, such a model would have to keep the discrimination
of a deep network while exposing what it reads from the ECG.
Block-term tensor models offer one route to such a model. A blockterm decomposition splits a multiway signal into blocks with low-rank factors
per mode [12]. In neural networks, block-term structure has compressed
weight matrices, and those networks are explained with Grad-CAM maps
instead of through their factors [13]. In supervised regression of brain signals,
block-term tensor regression, designed for interpretability, predicted finger
movements [14]. That regression is linear and has no survival objective,
which incident prediction with censored follow-up needs. To our knowledge,
no published model places readable block-term frequency and lead factors
on the predictive path of a survival network for incident AF. Such a model
would also need a way to add clinical risk factors, which complement the
ECG [6]. The clinical factors should not enter the factors that are meant to
be read, or the reading would mix the two sources.
1.3. Aim and contributions
This study addresses two questions. The first is whether a block-term
tensor network predicts incident AF from one sinus-rhythm 12-lead ECG
more accurately than a clinical risk-factor model and a convolutional neural
network (CNN). In this network, the ECG evidence passes through readable
frequency and lead factors. Age, sex and ECG intervals enter through a separate linear term. The second question is where in the cardiac cycle the ECG

did not show AF on expert review (Section 4.1) [15]. All methods share one
cohort, one set of folds and one set of risk factors. Differences between them

2. Related work
2.1. AI-ECG prediction of incident AF
Two tasks are reported under the heading of AF prediction from the sinusrhythm ECG, and they differ in what counts as a case. In identification, a
case is a patient who already has AF or shows it soon after the ECG. A CNN
developed on 649,931 sinus-rhythm ECGs of 180,922 Mayo Clinic patients,
454,789 of them in its training set, identified patients with AF with an AUC
of 0.87, counting AF before the tested ECG as positive [16]. A network for AF
within 31 days of a sinus-rhythm ECG reached AUROCs of 0.86 on held-out
Veterans Affairs ECGs and 0.93 at a non-VA site [17]. In incident prediction,
4

<U+000C>a case is a patient whose first AF follows the ECG after a stated interval.
The two tasks give different numbers. In a systematic review, models with
31-day confirmation windows had higher median AUCs than models with 1year windows [7], and a narrative review found AI more accurate at shorter
event horizons [8]. The present study addresses incident prediction only.
Studies of dated incident AF report lower values. Raghunath et al.
reached an AUROC of 0.85 for new-onset AF within one year on ECGs from
430,000 patients [5]. A model from a Montreal cardiac centre reached 0.78
at five years and 0.77 on external validation in MIMIC-IV [18]. Its abstract
does not state the label source used in MIMIC-IV. On the hospital from
which MIMIC-IV comes, a CNN trained with a discrete-time survival loss
reached a C-index of 0.750 [19]. A model of ECG images on the same hospital reached a C-statistic of 0.754 [20]. In Brazilian telehealth data, a residual
CNN with survival analysis reached an AUC of 0.845 [21], in the data family
of an earlier 12-lead diagnostic network [22]. A gradient-boosting survival
model of 417 engineered ECG features reached a C-index of 0.781, with a
mean follow-up of 80 days [23]. These designs differ in horizon, population
and label source, so their values do not rank the models.
The explanations in these studies are computed after training. Saliency
mapping gave the P-wave the greatest influence in one ECG network [6].
Saliency of the image model focused on P-wave morphology and the PR
interval [20]. The feature-based model named lower P-wave amplitude among
its predictors [23]. These reports agree on the atrial part of the beat. To our
knowledge, none of these reports includes a test of whether the explanation
reflects what the model uses to predict.
2.2. Clinical risk models and their combination with the ECG
Clinical risk models set the comparator that an ECG model has to match.
The Framingham score predicted 10-year AF from age, sex, a heart murmur,
heart failure, systolic blood pressure, hypertension treatment, body mass
index and the PR interval, with a C statistic of 0.78 [24]. CHARGE-AF
reached 0.765 at derivation and 0.664 and 0.705 in two validation cohorts [4].
Adding ECG variables did not improve its discrimination. ECG intervals
measured in sinus rhythm are nevertheless AF predictors in their own right.
In Framingham, each 20 ms increment of the PR interval carried an adjusted
hazard ratio of 1.12 for AF [25]. Across Framingham and ARIC, a P-wave
duration above 120 ms was associated with AF (hazard ratio 1.55), but Pwave indices added little discrimination beyond traditional risk factors [26].
5

<U+000C>These results motivate the ECG intervals in the clinical term of this study.
They also set a modest expectation for what interval measurements alone
add.
Studies that combined an ECG network with clinical factors found the
two complementary in most cohorts. AUROC was comparable for CHARGEAF and an ECG network in three test sets and highest for their combination,
at 0.838, 0.777 and 0.746 [6]. In an older community cohort, an AI-ECG and
CHARGE-AF had equal C statistics of 0.69, predicted AF independently,
and reached 0.72 together [27]. A single-lead ECG model with age and sex
had an AUROC of 0.695 against 0.679 for CHARGE-AF in trial data [28].
A random forest that combined demographic, clinical, engineered and deep
ECG features reached 0.909, against 0.839 for the best single modality [29].
Other studies found the ECG model ahead of the clinical score. In 2,301
patients of a Montreal cohort, an ECG model exceeded CHARGE-AF, which
reached an AUC of 0.62, and an AF polygenic score, which reached 0.59 [18].
An image-based ECG model reached 0.696 against 0.667 for CHARGE-AF,
and 0.711 when the two were combined [20]. The ranking of ECG and clinical
models also depends on the cohort. An ECG model led in hospital patients,
while CHARGE-AF was the best single predictor in UK Biobank [19]. Fusing
the two sources is therefore supported, but the way the fusion is built decides
whether the ECG part can still be read.
2.3. Interpretable models and post-hoc explanation
Post-hoc explanations have been put to tests, and several methods fail
them. Tests that randomise the model parameters and the training labels
showed some saliency methods to be independent of both the model and
the data-generating process [30]. Under remove-and-retrain evaluation, only
VarGrad and SmoothGrad-Squared, of the methods tested, did better than
a random assignment of importance [31]. On the ECG, twelve attribution
methods agreed poorly with one another [10]. Their self-consistency across
initialisations was moderate for most methods, with mean correlations of 0.41
to 0.65. Dataset-level auditing with sanity checks gives a more favourable
reading, in which aggregated saliency aligned with the decision rules of cardiologists [32]. In that work the same methods also supported knowledge
discovery, such as subtypes of myocardial infarction. The balance of this evidence leaves any explanation computed after training open to doubt about
what it shows. It motivates models whose readable parts lie on the path of
the prediction itself.
6

<U+000C>Such models route the prediction through parts that can be read. Concept bottleneck models first predict named concepts and then the label, at
accuracy competitive with end-to-end models [33]. Correcting the predicted
concepts at test time raised their accuracy, which shows one use of a readable intermediate layer. Their concept representations can, however, encode
information beyond the named concepts, which makes the reading of the
downstream prediction misleading [34]. B-cos networks summarise the full
computation as one linear transform at similar ImageNet accuracy [35]. An
anti-aliased variant gave explanations without artifacts on chest x-rays while
keeping its predictive performance [36]. The multilinear structure of tensor
networks has also been combined with deep networks, in preliminary results,
to give weight-based interpretability at accuracy equal to baselines [37]. The
leakage result bears on any nonlinear head placed on readable factors, including the head of the model in this study.
Whether intrinsic interpretability costs accuracy on the ECG is contested.
A study of variational-autoencoder representations of interpretable ECG features reported a cost of intrinsic explainability [38]. A supervised block-term
discriminant method reported none on EEG decoding, with a mean accuracy
of 64.52% against 61.00% for its predecessor, higher-order discriminant analysis, on motor imagery [39]. Image networks that are interpretable by design
also kept their accuracy [35, 36]. The answer may depend on the model family and on the task. To our knowledge, no study in this literature compares
an intrinsically interpretable model with a CNN on incident AF under one
protocol. That comparison is one aim of the present work.
2.4. Block-term tensor models
A block-term decomposition writes a tensor as a sum of blocks of low
multilinear rank, and it contains the CP and Tucker decompositions as special
cases [12]. A survey covers the CP and Tucker decompositions [40]. The uses
of block-term structure differ in where the factors sit: in the weights of a
network, within a recording, or on the input of a predictive model. Blockterm tensor neural networks approximate the weight matrices of CNNs and
recurrent networks by block-term tensors to compress them, and the networks
are explained with Grad-CAM maps instead of through their factors [13]. The
model of this paper shares part of that name and is a different model, whose
factors act on the input and can be read. On AF ECGs, a regularised blockterm decomposition extracted several signals interpretable as AF patterns
7

<U+000C>[41]. That work separates sources within a recording and does not predict
risk.
Supervised block-term models extract factors that both predict and can
be read. Block-term tensor regression (BTTR) predicted finger trajectories
from intracranial recordings, with automatic extraction of its components
[14]. A block-term discriminant analysis gave interpretable and efficient dimensionality reduction for brain-computer interfaces, with an ROC-AUC of
91.25% on event-related potentials [39]. Linear BTTR has also been applied
to the identification of AF in sinus rhythm on the median beat [42], which
is a different task from dated incidence. These models fit their blocks by deflation, score linearly, and have no time-to-event objective. No model in this
literature trains readable block-term frequency and lead factors end to end
with a survival objective for incident AF, with clinical factors kept outside
the factor layer.
3. Method
3.1. Spectral tensor and block-term layer
Each index ECG is a 10-second, 12-lead recording sampled at 500 Hz
[43]. Each lead was high-pass filtered at 0.5 Hz with a second-order filter and
notch filtered at 50 Hz. It was then denoised by soft thresholding of a threelevel sym4 wavelet decomposition, decimated to 250 Hz, centred, and scaled
by its maximum absolute value. A magnitude spectrogram was computed
per lead with a 128-sample window (512 ms) and a 32-sample hop (128
ms). Frequencies up to 40 Hz were kept, magnitudes were transformed by
log(1 + x), and the time axis was resampled to 40 frames. Mains interference
at 60 Hz, the mains frequency of the source hospital, therefore lies outside
the kept band. The result is a tensor X <element of> RF <multiplication sign>T <multiplication sign>L with F = 21 frequency
bins from 0 to 39 Hz, 1.95 Hz apart, T = 40 time frames and L = 12
leads, standardised per recording. Within each training fold, each lead was
standardised again with the statistics of the training patients. A magnitude
spectrogram changes little when the beat shifts within a window, so beats
need no alignment across recordings.

BTTN generalises linear BTTR [14]. Both models decompose the input
into blocks with one factor per mode. BTTR fits its blocks one at a time by
deflation, keeps a static score vector per block and predicts with a linear score.
BTTN trains all blocks jointly by gradient descent and replaces the static
score vector with learned temporal kernels. It pools their output, passes it
through a nonlinear head and is fitted with a Cox survival objective. Figure 1
shows the two models one above the other. The frequency and lead factors
keep the same role in both models, so the factors of BTTN can be read as
those of BTTR can.
3.2. Temporal convolution, pooling, head and survival objective
The 72 time series of the block-term layer pass through depthwise temporal convolutions at three scales. Each series is convolved with six kernels
of each of the lengths 3, 7 and 15 frames, so that each output channel depends on one series of one block. Batch normalisation and a rectified linear
unit follow. Each channel is then pooled along time in two ways: by one
set of attention weights over time, computed from all channels by a softmax and shared by them, and by its maximum. The two pooled vectors are
concatenated. A head with two hidden layers of 256 and 128 units, batch
normalisation, rectified linear units and dropout of 0.4 maps the pooled vector to a scalar log-risk <greek small letter eta>ECG . This network without clinical factors is the
ECG term of BTTN, called ECG-only BTTN when it is evaluated alone.

3.3. Clinical term and readable factors
The clinical term is a linear function of nine risk factors. They are age,
sex, and seven automated measurements of the index ECG from MIMIC-IVECG. The RR interval and the P, QRS and T axes are used as released. The
P-wave duration, QRS duration and QT interval were computed from the
released onset and end times of the waves. Age is the anchor age of MIMICIV [46]. The factors are standardised with the statistics of the training fold,
and a missing value is replaced by the training mean. The term w<down tack> c has no
intercept and is added to the log-risk of the ECG term:

The weights w are initialised from a class-balanced logistic regression on the
training split. They are excluded from weight decay, so that training does
not shrink them towards zero. The PR interval is not among the factors. We
call BTTN with the clinical term the fused model.
This construction makes the factors readable by design and keeps the
clinical term out of them. In this paper, readable names this property of the
factors of a model that is interpretable by design. It is the sense in which
the title calls BTTN interpretable. Every path from the ECG to the risk
passes through the block projections of Equation (1). The frequency and
lead factors are therefore parameters on which the prediction depends, and
they can be read directly. The clinical term reaches the log-risk without
passing through the block-term layer. Adding it leaves the role of the factors
unchanged, and the risk factors can be read as a linear term beside the ECG
evidence. Readability is here a property of the architecture, and this study
reports no reading of the learned frequency and lead factors. Whether the
nonlinear head uses the factors in the way a reader of them would assume is
a separate question, which this study does not measure [34].

<U+000C>4. Experiments
4.1. Cohort and labels
The cohort comes from MIMIC-IV-ECG, about 800,000 diagnostic ECGs
from nearly 160,000 patients [43]. It is linked to the hospital module of
MIMIC-IV, drawn from the electronic health record of the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center [46]. Incident AF is the first hospital admission with
a diagnosis of AF or flutter (ICD-10 I48, or ICD-9 427.31 or 427.32 for older
admissions), dated by its admission time. AF and flutter were counted together, since a coded flutter diagnosis did not reliably separate flutter from
AF in a registry validation [47]. Hospital diagnoses were preferred to labels
read from the ECG, since only 65.4% of 700 AF-labelled ECGs in MIMIC-IV
showed AF on expert review [15].
The index ECG is each patient<right single quotation mark>s earliest ECG whose machine report
reads sinus rhythm, without AF, flutter, or a paced, junctional or ectopic
atrial rhythm. A patient was excluded as prevalent or concurrent when an
AF diagnosis, or an ECG read as AF, fell before the end of a 30-day blanking
window after the index ECG. Follow-up ends at the later of the last ECG
and death. In the one-year design, cases had onset more than 30 and at most
365 days after the index ECG. Controls had no AF diagnosis and at least
365 days of follow-up. The survival design kept onset at any time after the
blanking window, with the same controls. For modelling, the one-year design
used all 1,855 cases and four controls per case, drawn from 16,000 sampled
controls, for 9,275 patients by design. The survival design used 4,000 sampled
events and 16,000 sampled controls, 20,000 patients in all.
4.2. Baselines, protocol and metrics
Three baselines shared the cohort, folds and inputs of BTTN. The clinical
model is a class-balanced logistic regression on the nine factors of the clinical
term, with median imputation and standardisation. Linear BTTR [14] was
fitted on the same spectral tensor to the label coded as <minus sign>1 and +1, with up
to eight blocks. The CNN applies three one-dimensional convolutional layers
of 32, 64 and 64 channels along time, with frequency and lead flattened
into input channels. With batch normalisation, dropout and global average
pooling, it is trained with weighted cross-entropy and early stopping as a
three-member ensemble. In the survival design, the clinical baseline is a Cox
model with a ridge penalty of 0.1 on the same factors.

12

<U+000C>The one-year comparison used stratified five-fold cross-validation at the
patient level, repeated over five seeds, with each patient<right single quotation mark>s scores averaged
over seeds. The survival model used one stratified five-fold split, with an
event within one year as the label for early stopping. Each patient<right single quotation mark>s risk in
the survival design came from the fold in which that patient was held out.
The clinical Cox model was fitted on the same training folds as the networks.
AUROC intervals come from 2,000 bootstrap resamples with percentile limits. At each horizon, cases had an event by the horizon and controls were
AF-free beyond it, and patients censored earlier were excluded. Calibration
and clinical use were assessed at one year in the survival sample, after twofold cross-fitted logistic recalibration. They were measured by Brier scores,
decision curves [48] and operating points at the top 5, 10, 20 and 30% of
risk. Positive predictive value (PPV) and the number needed to monitor, its
inverse, were recomputed at the proportion of cases among the cases and AFfree controls of the one-year design, 1,855 in 43,625. We report the study with
reference to the TRIPOD+AI statement [49], without a table of participant
characteristics and without counts of missing values per clinical factor.
4.3. Occlusion readout
Occlusion sensitivity covers part of the input and records the change in the
output [50]. It is one of the perturbation-based explanation methods [51].
We applied it to a single ECG-only BTTN trained on the whole survival
sample. For the 120 highest-risk ECGs of patients with an event, a window
of 12 samples (24 ms at 500 Hz) was set to zero in every beat of one lead. The
window ran from 6 samples before to 5 samples after a fixed offset from each
R peak. Each offset was rounded to a whole sample towards the R peak. R
peaks were detected on lead II. The spectrogram was rebuilt and the drop in
log-risk recorded, for each lead and for offsets from 350 ms before to 425 ms
after the R peak in steps of 25 ms. Neighbouring windows abut or leave one
sample between them. Zeroing a high-energy part of the beat changes the
spectrogram most. Each mean drop was therefore also divided by the sum of
two terms: the mean energy of the removed samples, and, for stability, one
tenth of that energy<right single quotation mark>s mean over all leads and offsets.
The offsets from 225 ms before to 375 ms after the R peak were grouped
into five windows, the P, PR, QRS, ST and T windows. Table 5 gives the
offsets each window contains and the span of the beat that their occlusions
cover. The offsets from 350 to 250 ms before the R peak and at 400 and
425 ms after it belong to no window. They are shown in Figure 3, and

5.3. Calibration, risk stratification and operating points
After logistic recalibration within the survival sample, predicted and observed one-year proportions of the fused model were close in most deciles,
among 19,986 patients with 838 cases (Figure 2). The largest gap lay in the
sixth decile (bar 5 of Figure 2), 4.95% observed against a mean prediction
of 3.56%. The deciles are read in the survival sample, which holds a larger
share of the events of the cohort than of its controls (4,000 of 8,775 against
16,000 of 41,770). Its proportions of AF are therefore higher than those of
the cohort. Observed one-year AF rose from 0.55% in the lowest decile to
11.4% in the highest, with mean predicted risks of 0.61% and 12.3%. The
sixth decile also had a higher observed proportion than the seventh (bar 6,
4.15%). Brier scores were 0.0390 for the fused model, 0.0396 for ECG-only
BTTN and 0.0398 for the clinical model.
At the top 10% of risk, the fused model had a sensitivity of 0.272 against
0.240 for the clinical model, at specificities of 0.908 and 0.906 (Table 4). At
the proportion of cases in the one-year design, its PPV was 0.1155 against
0.1019, a number needed to monitor of 8.7 against 9.8. Sensitivity and PPV
were higher for the fused model than for the clinical model at all four flagged
fractions.

5.4. Cardiac-cycle occlusion profile
Raw occlusion of the ECG term was dominated by the QRS window,
where the mean drop in log-risk was 0.0232 against 0.0037 in the PR window
(Table 5). The QRS complex has the highest amplitude of the beat, so zeroing
it removes the most energy and changes the spectrogram most. Per unit of
energy removed, the profile was highest in the PR window, at 0.00216. The
ST window (0.00157), the P window (0.00152) and the T window (0.00127)
followed, and the QRS window was lowest (0.00086). The P and ST windows
were close to each other. Figure 3 shows the energy-normalised response on
the mean median beat of each lead. The profile describes what the fitted
ECG term responds to on 120 high-risk training ECGs.

6. Discussion
6.1. Comparison with published incident-AF models
These results answer the research question within one health system.
BTTN with the clinical term had the highest one-year discrimination of the
methods compared. Its point estimates stayed above those of the clinical
model at four horizons, read from the same cross-validated risks. Its ECG
term alone was comparable to a CNN under the same protocol. After logistic
recalibration within the survival sample, the predicted and observed one-year
proportions of the fused model agreed closely in most risk deciles. It flagged
more future cases than the clinical model at each flagged fraction. Per unit
of energy, its ECG term responded most to the PR window of the beat. The
gain of the fused model over the clinical model is small and consistent in
direction. The one-year intervals overlap, and the study reports no interval
of the difference that would establish it.
Published results give context for these values. On the hospital behind
MIMIC-IV, a survival-loss CNN reached a C-index of 0.750 [19] and an imagebased model 0.754 [20]. A Montreal model reached an AUROC of 0.77 on
external validation in MIMIC-IV, with a label source that its abstract does
not state [18]. Elsewhere, an AUROC of 0.85 at one year [5] and a C-index of
0.781 over a short follow-up [23] were reported. These studies differ from the
present one in horizon, outcome definition, sample and metric. A systematic
review shows how far such choices move reported values [7]. Identification
studies [16, 17] address another task. So does AF diagnosis from the ECG:
21

<U+000C>a foundation model reported an AUROC of 0.996 for the AF label of multilabel ECG interpretation, and its authors released an open benchmark on
MIMIC-IV-ECG [54].
The gain of the fused model over the clinical model agrees with reports
that ECG and clinical information are complementary [6, 27]. Its operating
point at the top 10% of risk is of the same order as that of one externally validated model. That model flagged 9.7% of older patients with a sensitivity of
31% and a specificity of 92%, in a different population with chart-confirmed
endpoints [55]. The comparability of ECG-only BTTN with the CNN bears
on the reported accuracy cost of intrinsic interpretability on the ECG [38].
In this design, the ECG term built on readable factors reached the discrimination of a compact CNN. The CNN has three convolutional layers, so the
result says nothing about parity with the large networks of published work.
6.2. Implications of an ECG term built on readable factors, with a separate
clinical term
Separating the clinical term from the block-term layer lets the fused model
gain discrimination without passing the risk factors through its ECG factors.
The ECG evidence still passes through frequency and lead factors, and each
risk factor enters the linear term with one weight. The two sources therefore
have separate parameters that can be inspected apart, although this study
reports no reading of the fitted frequency and lead factors. The fused model
is trained as one network, so its factors are fitted beside the clinical term and
may differ from those of ECG-only BTTN. In a fused network whose clinical
inputs enter the factor layer, the factors could absorb risk-factor information,
as concept representations absorb information beyond their concepts [34].
The architecture of BTTN closes this route for the clinical term. It does not
close it for information that the ECG itself carries, which the factors may
encode in ways a reader does not expect.
The PR-window concentration of the occlusion profile is in line with
saliency reports of other incident-AF models. These pointed to the P-wave
[6] and to P-wave morphology and the PR interval [20]. It is also in line with
cohort studies that tie the PR interval measured in sinus rhythm to later
AF. In Framingham, the PR interval was a predictor in the AF risk score
[24], and first-degree atrioventricular block carried an adjusted hazard ratio
of 2.06 [25]. Each standard deviation of the PR interval carried a hazard
ratio of 1.13 in older Black and White adults [56]. First-degree block carried
a hazard ratio of 1.75 in an older Japanese population [57]. In Black adults of
22

<U+000C>the Jackson Heart Study, the PR interval, P-wave duration and P-wave terminal force were each associated with AF [58]. P-wave indices show the same
direction in a two-cohort study [26] and in a meta-analysis whose evidence
was of very low certainty in selected populations [59].
The association is not uniform, and the comparison has a limited scope.
In a Swedish cohort of 70-year-olds, the PR interval measured in lead V1
was not related to incident AF, while a short P-wave duration was [60]. The
cohort studies measure the PR interval from the onset of the P wave to
the onset of the QRS complex. The PR window of the readout groups the
occlusions centred 125 to 75 ms before the R peak, a fixed span from 136
to 62 ms before it. On the mean beat of Figure 3, this span holds the end
of the P wave and the PR segment. The agreement claimed here therefore
concerns the location within the cardiac cycle only. The clinical term holds
P-wave duration but no PR interval. One reading of the gain of the fused
model is that the ECG term carries information on atrial and atrioventricular
conduction that the clinical term does not measure. The present results do
not test this reading, which remains a hypothesis.
An atrial focus also has a clinical frame. An expert consensus defines
atrial cardiomyopathy as structural, architectural, contractile or electrophysiological changes of the atria that can produce clinically relevant manifestations, among them arrhythmia [61]. In a Japanese community cohort, prolonged P-wave duration was associated both with incident AF and with more
left atrial fibrosis at autopsy [62]. One reading of the PR-window response is
that it reflects such atrial change. The readout cannot show this. The reading
is offered as a hypothesis for studies that pair the ECG with measurements
of the atrium.
6.3. Limitations
The study used one database from one health system and has no external
validation in a cohort with dated outcomes. Its labels are ICD-coded hospital diagnoses, which are mostly correct but incomplete. A meta-analysis of
24 validation studies pooled a PPV of 88% and a sensitivity of 80% for administrative AF definitions [63]. In a population study, a hospital discharge
diagnosis found 73.7% of record-verified AF, with a PPV of 88.5% [64]. A
registry found a PPV of 92.6% for the combined AF or flutter diagnosis [47].
Some controls may therefore have AF diagnosed only outside hospital. Onset
is dated by the admission and not by the first occurrence of AF. Follow-up
to death counts patients without a hospital AF diagnosis as AF-free.
23

<U+000C>The comparator and the covariates have limits of their own. Age is the
anchor age of MIMIC-IV, which is not the age at the index ECG [46]. The
clinical factors omit variables of the Framingham score, among them systolic
blood pressure, hypertension treatment, body mass index and heart failure
[24]. They also omit the PR interval, a known AF predictor [25, 56]. The
clinical comparator is therefore narrower than a full clinical score. The CNN
is compact, and a larger network might reach a different value.
The evaluation of calibration and operating points has limits as well. The
probabilities were recalibrated in the evaluation sample with two-fold crossfitting, so the calibration of the model in another population is not shown.
The one-year design sampled four controls per case, and the survival design
sampled cases and controls. PPV was therefore recomputed at the proportion
of cases among the cases and AF-free controls of the one-year design, 1,855
in 43,625. This proportion exceeds the one-year rate of the survival cohort,
1,855 in 50,545, whose one-year negatives include the patients with onset
after one year. The PPVs of Table 4 are therefore upper values for that
population. The study also reports no table of the characteristics of cases
and controls and no count of missing values per clinical factor. The sample
therefore cannot be compared with another population on these measures.
The occlusion readout is a perturbation explanation of the fitted model
on 120 high-risk training ECGs. It is open to the critiques of post-hoc attribution [30, 31, 10]. Its zero replacement, its 24 ms window and its fixed
windows relative to the R peak shape the profile, since the replacement value
and the window size shape an occlusion map [52]. The perturbation method
and the region size likewise shape the evaluation of attributions on time series
[53]. Another replacement could change the order of the windows. Readable
factors do not by themselves show that the nonlinear head routes its decision through them in the way their reading suggests [34]. The readability of
BTTN rests on its architecture, and this study reports no measurement of
how far the factors carry the decision on ICD-coded labels.
6.4. Future research
Several studies would extend this work. A test of the block factors on
ICD-coded labels would measure how far the factors carry the decision. It
would use control probes and the removal of top-ranked against bottomranked factors, in the manner of remove-and-retrain evaluation [31]. External
validation in a cohort with dated outcomes, and temporal validation within
MIMIC-IV, would test whether the model transports. Such validation would
24

<U+000C>be reported with reference to the TRIPOD+AI statement [49]. A clinical
comparator that includes the PR interval and the variables of a full clinical
score would sharpen the comparison with the clinical model. A paired interval for the difference between the fused and the clinical model would show
whether the one-year gain exceeds sampling variation. An occlusion readout
on held-out ECGs, with replacements other than zero, would show how far
the PR-window profile depends on the choices of the present readout.
7. Conclusion
On ICD-coded hospital diagnoses linked to MIMIC-IV-ECG, a block-term
tensor network with a separate clinical term had the highest one-year discrimination of the methods compared. Its AUROC was 0.7776, against 0.7597 for
a clinical model and 0.7350 for a CNN. Without refitting for each horizon,
it kept its point estimates above those of the clinical model at horizons from
120 days to five years after the index ECG. After logistic recalibration within
the survival sample, the predicted and observed one-year proportions of the
fused model agreed closely in most risk deciles. The fused model also flagged
more future cases than the clinical model at each flagged fraction. Its gain
over the clinical model is small, and the one-year intervals overlap. The gain
is consistent in direction within one health system, and no interval for the
difference yet establishes it.
The ECG term alone reached the discrimination of a compact CNN under
the same protocol, while its evidence passes through frequency and lead
factors that lie on the predictive path and can be read. The clinical term
adds the risk factors without passing through those factors. Per unit of signal
energy, the ECG term responded most to the PR window of the beat. This is
the part of the cardiac cycle that cohort studies of the PR interval tie to later
AF [25, 56]. The readout describes the fitted model, and its atrial reading
remains a hypothesis.
For the design of ECG risk models, the results suggest that a clinical term
kept outside the block-term factors adds to the discrimination of the ECG
term. At one year, the fused model reached 0.7776 [0.7664, 0.7888] and the
ECG term alone 0.7397 [0.7275, 0.7517]. These two intervals do not overlap,
although the study reports no paired interval for this difference. The clinical
comparator used here is narrower than a full clinical score, and the readout
is descriptive. Validation outside one health system, a test of the factors on

25

<U+000C>ICD-coded labels and a comparator that includes the PR interval are the
next requirements before any clinical use.
